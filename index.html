The problem

Smart bottles keep solving the wrong thing.

Most of them add a screen and a subscription to a container that already worked. Then they fail in the three places that actually matter.

  1. 01

    The battery dies when you need it

    A bottle that needs charging every few days is a bottle you stop carrying. Halfway up a trail, a flat battery turns a smart bottle into a heavy dumb one.

  2. 02

    MicroUSB, in the year 2026

    An exposed port on something you fill with water is a design decision that has to be defended. Most of them use a connector the rest of the industry retired years ago.

  3. 03

    They tell you it's cold. They don't keep it warm.

    Reading the temperature is the easy half. Holding it — through a two-hour practice, in the cold — is the half that takes real insulation and real thermal engineering.

What's built in

Engineered around how you actually drink.

Every control is something you can operate one-handed, with cold hands, without looking at a screen.

  • The ring sits under the cap's outer edge, so the light washes across the lid rather than shining at you. Tap once and it reads the current water temperature and lights the arc that corresponds to it.

    It stays lit only long enough to read, then fades. That matters more than it sounds: an always-on display is the single biggest drain on every smart bottle we looked at, and it's the reason theirs are flat by Thursday.

  • The bezel clicks through fixed positions rather than sweeping freely, so you can count detents by feel and know where you landed without looking. With gloves on, in the dark, that's the difference between a control you use and one you don't.

    Because the target is stored mechanically on the bottle, there is no pairing step, no account, and nothing to re-sync when you change phones. The bottle behaves the same whether your phone is in your pocket or at home.

  • A magnetic puck lands on the base and self-aligns, so there's no orientation to get right. The contacts sit flush with the housing, which means there is no cavity for grit, sweat or drink mix to collect in.

    This is the part of the design we're least willing to compromise on. An exposed MicroUSB port on a container you fill with water is a failure waiting to happen, and it's the most common thing wrong with the bottles already on sale.

  • The latch sits on the side of the lid, where your thumb already is when you pick the bottle up. One flick opens the spout — no twisting, and nothing to put down first.

    The catch works in two stages: the first holds the spout shut against knocks in a bag, and only a deliberate second movement releases it. That's what stops it opening against your kit while you're running.

    It's a rocker rather than a sprung push-button on purpose. Springs are the part that goes soft after a season of hard use, and a latch that no longer holds is worse than no latch at all.

  • UV-C is genuinely effective at treating water, and genuinely bad for eyes and skin. So the lamp is gated behind two sensors that work on completely different principles: a light sensor that confirms the chamber is dark, and a magnetic sensor that confirms the cap is physically seated.

    Both have to agree before the lamp will fire, and either one disagreeing mid-cycle cuts it immediately. Two different sensing methods means a single failure — a stuck sensor, a spoofed reading, a cracked lens — can't be enough to expose anyone.

    This is the feature that made the rest of the bottle worth building. Safe drinking water is the reason it exists.

  • A two-second hold on the lid — deliberately longer than the tap that reads temperature, so you can't trigger it by accident — runs a short active warm-up.

    Here is the honest version: heating liquid takes real energy, and a bottle you carry all day can only hold so much battery. Boost takes the chill off. It will not make a cold drink hot, and any bottle claiming otherwise is either carrying a battery you wouldn't want to hike with or isn't telling you the truth.

    The insulation does the heavy lifting. Boost is for the top-up.

Why this exists

A note from the founder

I'm 12, based in India, and I play football, badminton, pickleball and cricket. In 2025 I got a bad case of typhoid from contaminated water, and it got me thinking about how something as basic as safe drinking water isn't a given for everyone.

Around the same time I was getting frustrated with my own bottle mid-practice — dead batteries, cold coffee, apps I never open. Both problems led to the same answer.

I designed the full spec myself: features, materials, cost structure. My parent is co-applicant, since I'm under 18.
